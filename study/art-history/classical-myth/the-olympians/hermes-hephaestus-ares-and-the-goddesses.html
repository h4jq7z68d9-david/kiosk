The Olympians

Hermes, Hephaestus, Ares, and the Goddesses

Messengers, makers, lovers, and the powers of women

This part covers the remaining gods and the five great goddesses. Hermes moves between worlds as messenger, trickster, and guide of the dead; Hephaestus makes things; Ares brings violence. The goddesses each govern part of women’s lives and the fertility of the world: Demeter the crops, Hestia the house, Aphrodite attraction, Artemis wild animals and girls, Athena weaving and skill.

  • Hermes: boundaries, trade, the dead
  • Hephaestus and Ares: craft and violence
  • Goddesses mapped onto women’s lives
Female Olympians and fertility
Each goddess oversees one kind of growth or plenty: Demeter the grain harvest, Hestia the household, Aphrodite sexual desire, Artemis wild animals, and Athena weaving and other crafts.

Miniature herm of HermesAfter Alkamenes · Roman, 1st–2nd century CE, after a Greek original of ca. 430 BCEHermes, son of Zeus and the nymph Maia, protects travelers, merchants, and thieves, and guides souls of the dead to the underworld. Greeks set up herms, pillars topped with his bearded head, outside houses and at crossroads to ward off harm. This old-fashioned, bearded style copies a famous herm by the sculptor Alkamenes that stood at the entrance to the Athenian Acropolis. Echo and NarcissusJohn William Waterhouse · 1903Hermes’s son Pan, half man and half goat, is the god of shepherds and wild places; sudden fear in lonely country was blamed on him, giving us the word “panic.” In one story Pan pursued the nymph Echo, who rejected him and was torn apart until only her voice remained. In Ovid’s better-known version, shown here, Echo pines for Narcissus, who falls in love with his own reflection. Venus and MarsSandro Botticelli · ca. 1485Aphrodite’s husband was Hephaestus, the lame smith whose forge was on the island of Lemnos, but she loved Ares. In the Odyssey, Hephaestus catches the lovers in an invisible net and calls the gods to laugh at them. Botticelli shows a calmer moral: Mars sleeps, exhausted, while Venus stays alert and little satyrs play with his armor. Love conquers war. Aphrodite of Melos (Venus de Milo)Hellenistic, ca. 150–125 BCEAphrodite was said to have come ashore on Cyprus, and her great sanctuary stood at Paphos. Her cult has Near Eastern roots in the love-and-war goddesses Inanna, Ishtar, and Astarte, and ancient writers claimed some of her temples practiced sacred prostitution, a claim modern scholars debate. The statue’s lost arms make it impossible to know whether she held an apple, a mirror, or a shield. Sappho and AlcaeusLawrence Alma-Tadema · 1881Sappho, who lived on the island of Lesbos around 600 BCE, was the most celebrated female poet of antiquity, and her surviving “Hymn to Aphrodite” calls on the goddess for help in love. Alma-Tadema imagines her listening as the poet Alcaeus plays the lyre. Her poems about desire between women are why “lesbian” comes from Lesbos. Venus and AdonisTitian · 1550sAdonis was born from a tree: his mother Myrrha (or Smyrna), after an incestuous passion for her father, King Cinyras of Cyprus, was turned into the myrrh tree. Aphrodite loved the beautiful young hunter and begged him not to hunt, but he was killed by a boar, and anemones sprang from his blood. Titian invents the moment of her clinging plea as he pulls away. Women mourned him each year in the festival of the Adonia. Pygmalion and GalateaJean-Léon Gérôme · ca. 1890In Ovid, the Cypriot sculptor Pygmalion carved an ivory woman so beautiful that he fell in love with her, and Aphrodite answered his prayer by bringing the statue to life. Their child was Paphos, ancestor of King Cinyras. The name Galatea for the statue was added only in the 1700s. Gérôme shows the marble turning to flesh from the top down as she bends to kiss him. Sleeping HermaphroditusRoman copy, 2nd century CE, of a Hellenistic originalHermaphroditus, the son of Hermes and Aphrodite, was so beautiful that the water nymph Salmacis begged the gods to join them forever, and their bodies fused into one with both male and female features. Another son of Aphrodite, Priapus (by Dionysus or Hermes), was a garden god of fertility. Bernini carved the startlingly soft mattress when the statue belonged to Cardinal Scipione Borghese. Venus and AnchisesEngraving after Annibale Carracci · print after a fresco of 1597–1608Zeus made Aphrodite herself fall for a mortal, the Trojan shepherd-prince Anchises. As the Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite tells, she visited him disguised as a girl and later revealed herself, warning him never to boast of it. Their son was Aeneas, the hero who would escape Troy and found the Roman people. Carracci’s fresco shows Anchises removing her sandal.

Theme

Desire as a dangerous power

Aphrodite’s gifts bring joy, ruin, and transformation.

Aphrodite and Eros can overpower anyone, even Zeus. Their stories end in transformation as often as happiness: Myrrha becomes a tree, Adonis a flower, Hermaphroditus a double body, a statue a living woman. For the Greeks, desire was a force of nature like the sea, beautiful and creative but impossible to control.

  • Even gods fall under Aphrodite’s power
  • Love stories end in transformation
  • Desire as an uncontrollable force

Artemis of EphesusRoman copy of a Hellenistic original of the 2nd century BCEAt Ephesus, in Asia Minor, Artemis was worshipped as a mother goddess very unlike the slim huntress of mainland Greece. Her statue is covered with rows of rounded forms, long called breasts but possibly eggs or bulls’ parts, along with animals and bees, all symbols of fertility. The Ephesian Artemis shows how a Greek name could attach to an older local goddess. Niobe and Her Youngest DaughterRoman copy, 2nd century CE, of a Greek group of the 4th century BCEQueen Niobe of Thebes boasted that her fourteen children made her greater than Leto, who had only two. Leto’s twins, Apollo and Artemis, shot down all of Niobe’s children with arrows. The grieving mother was turned to stone on Mount Sipylus, still weeping as a spring. Here she shelters her youngest daughter and looks up toward the archers. Diana and ActaeonTitian · 1556–59The hunter Actaeon stumbled on Artemis bathing with her nymphs. Furious that a man had seen her naked, she turned him into a stag, and his own hounds tore him apart. Titian shows the instant of discovery: Actaeon pulls back a curtain as the goddess snatches a cloth and glares. The stag skull on the column predicts his fate. The ParthenonIktinos and Kallikrates, with Phidias · 447–432 BCEAthens built the Parthenon after the Persian Wars, on the Acropolis the Persians had burned. Its sculptures showed Athena’s birth, her contest with Poseidon for Athens, and battles of order against chaos. Inside stood Phidias’s colossal gold-and-ivory statue of the goddess. The building became the model of Classical architecture for more than two thousand years. Varvakeion AthenaAfter Phidias · Roman copy, ca. 200–250 CE, of the Athena Parthenos (438 BCE)Phidias’s Athena Parthenos was about 40 feet tall, built of gold and ivory over a wooden core, and none of it survives. This small copy, found near the Varvakeion school in Athens, preserves its main features: a triple-crested helmet, the aegis with the Gorgon’s head, a figure of Victory in her right hand, and a shield and serpent at her side. “Snake Goddess”Minoan, ca. 1600 BCEThis small figure holds a snake in each hand, with a cat perched on her head. Some scholars see Minoan goddesses like her as distant ancestors of Athena, who also has a sacred snake and a protective role over a city. Arthur Evans’s team restored it heavily, and parts, including the head and cat, may be modern. The Spinners (The Fable of Arachne)Diego Velázquez · ca. 1655–60The Lydian weaver Arachne boasted that she wove better than Athena. In their contest, she wove the gods’ scandalous love affairs so perfectly that Athena struck her, and Arachne was turned into a spider. Velázquez puts working women spinning in the foreground and the myth in the bright room behind, where the tapestry copies Titian’s Rape of Europa.

Theme

Goddesses of women’s lives

Each goddess governs a stage or skill of a woman’s life.

Artemis protects girls before marriage, Aphrodite rules the attraction that leads to it, Hera guards marriage itself, Demeter motherhood and the harvest, Hestia the household fire, and Athena the weaving every woman was expected to master. Myths like Arachne’s or Niobe’s warn women against pride, just as Marsyas’s warns men.

  • Artemis: girlhood; Aphrodite: attraction
  • Hera and Demeter: marriage and motherhood
  • Athena and Hestia: weaving and home
