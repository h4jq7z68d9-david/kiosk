The Olympians

Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, and Apollo

The divine family, the loves of Zeus, and the god of Delphi

The Greeks imagined their gods as a family that looks and behaves like a human one, only immortal and far more powerful. Zeus is the father who keeps order, Hera the mother who guards marriage, and their brothers and children each control part of the world. Most stories about Zeus are about his unions with goddesses and mortal women, which produce the forces of the universe and the heroes of legend. Apollo, his son by Leto, stands for prophecy, music, healing, and order.

  • Gods as an immortal human family
  • Zeus’s unions create cosmic powers and heroes
  • Apollo: prophecy, music, healing, plague
Anthropomorphism
Picturing gods in human form with human feelings; the basis of nearly all Greek religious art.
Xenia
Guest-friendship, the sacred duty between host and guest that Zeus protects; breaking it brings disaster.
Metis
Titaness of cleverness, Zeus’s first consort, mother of Athena.
Themis
Titaness of divine law and order; by Zeus mother of the Horae (Seasons) and the Moirae (Fates).
Moirae (Fates)
Clotho, who spins the thread of life; Lachesis, who measures it; and Atropos, who cuts it.
Eurynome
An Oceanid, by Zeus mother of the three Graces.
Mnemosyne
Titaness of memory, by Zeus mother of the nine Muses.

Theme

A family of gods

Greek gods quarrel, love, and scheme like a human family.

Each Olympian has a family role and a part of the world: Zeus the father and judge, Hera the wife and mother, Poseidon the sea, Hestia the hearth, Demeter the harvest, Hephaestus the crafts, Athena women’s skills and practical wisdom, Aphrodite desire, Apollo prophecy and healing, Hermes travel and messages. Because the gods act like people, their stories could model, or warn against, human behavior.

  • Each god has a domain and a family role
  • Human passions on a cosmic scale
  • Myths as models and warnings

DanaëTitian · ca. 1560–65King Acrisius of Argos locked his daughter Danaë in a bronze chamber because an oracle said her son would kill him. Zeus reached her as a shower of gold, and she gave birth to the hero Perseus. Titian contrasts the relaxed, glowing princess with her old nurse, who greedily catches the coins in her apron. Leda and the SwanAttributed to Francesco Melzi, after Leonardo da Vinci · ca. 1505–07Zeus took the form of a swan to seduce Leda, queen of Sparta. From the eggs she laid came Helen, later of Troy, and in most versions her sister Clytemnestra and the twins Castor and Pollux. Leonardo’s original is lost, so copies like this one preserve his twisting standing figure and the children hatching at her feet. The Rape of EuropaTitian · 1560–62Zeus turned himself into a beautiful white bull, coaxed the Phoenician princess Europa onto his back, and swam with her across the sea to Crete. There she bore Minos, the future king of Crete, whose palace and Labyrinth return in the story of Theseus. Titian shows her sprawled and terrified as the shore recedes, with cupids riding the waves. The Abduction of GanymedeCorreggio · ca. 1531–32Zeus, in the form of an eagle, snatched up the beautiful Trojan prince Ganymede and carried him to Olympus to be the gods’ cupbearer. Correggio paints the boy rising into the sky while his dog barks below. The Romans called him Catamitus, the source of the English word “catamite,” because the story was read as Zeus’s love for a youth. Jupiter and IoCorreggio · ca. 1531–32To hide his affair with the priestess Io from Hera, Zeus wrapped himself in a dark cloud; Correggio shows only a faint face and a hand emerging from the mist as Io melts into his embrace. When Hera grew suspicious, Zeus turned Io into a white cow, and Hera set the hundred-eyed giant Argus to guard her. Mercury and ArgusDiego Velázquez · ca. 1659Zeus sent Hermes to free Io. Hermes lulled the watchman Argus Panoptes (“all-seeing”) to sleep with music and stories, then killed him. Hera placed Argus’s hundred eyes on the tail of her peacock. Velázquez paints a quiet, shadowy moment as Hermes creeps toward the dozing guard, with the cow behind them.

Theme

The loves of Zeus

Zeus’s many unions explain where heroes and ruling families came from.

Zeus takes shape after shape, a shower of gold, a swan, a bull, an eagle, a cloud, to reach the women and youths he desires. To ancient audiences these stories gave great families and cities a divine ancestor: Perseus, Helen, and Minos all descend from such unions. Renaissance rulers commissioned these scenes as elegant, erotic pictures, which is why so many survive.

  • Disguises: gold, swan, bull, eagle, cloud
  • Divine ancestors for heroes and kings
  • Favorite subject of Renaissance patrons

The Garden of the HesperidesFrederic Leighton · 1892When Hera married Zeus, Earth gave her a tree of golden apples, which grew in a garden at the western edge of the world. The nymphs called the Hesperides tended it, guarded by the serpent Ladon, near where Atlas holds up the sky. Leighton’s drowsy nymphs and coiled serpent prepare for the later labor in which Heracles takes the apples. Neptune and AmphitriteJan Gossaert · 1516Poseidon, the Roman Neptune, married the sea nymph Amphitrite, and their son was the merman Triton. Gossaert, who had just seen ancient sculpture in Rome, poses the couple as massive classical nudes inside an ornate temple. Poseidon also fathered monsters and marvels: the Cyclops Polyphemus with the nymph Thoosa, and, with Medusa, the winged horse Pegasus and the warrior Chrysaor. The CyclopsOdilon Redon · ca. 1914Polyphemus, son of Poseidon and the nymph Thoosa, is the one-eyed giant Odysseus later blinds. In a story told by Theocritus and Ovid, he falls hopelessly in love with the sea nymph Galatea. Redon shows the giant peering shyly over a hill at the sleeping nymph, turning a monster into a lonely, almost gentle figure. Cup with King Aegeus consulting the oracleAttributed to the Kodros Painter · Athenian, ca. 440–430 BCEThe Athenian king Aegeus, childless, asks the oracle what to do; the answer will lead to the birth of Theseus. The prophetess, labeled Themis here, sits on Apollo’s tall tripod holding a laurel sprig and a dish. At Delphi the priestess called the Pythia spoke Apollo’s answers, and priests turned them into verse. Temple of Apollo at Delphi4th century BCE (rebuilt after 373 BCE)Apollo was born on the island of Delos to the Titaness Leto, after Hera banned every land from receiving her and held back the birth goddess Eileithyia. As a young god he killed the serpent Python at Delphi and took over the site. His temple carried two famous mottoes, “Know yourself” and “Nothing in excess.” Anyone stained by bloodshed had to be purified before approaching him. Apollo and DaphneGian Lorenzo Bernini · 1622–25Struck by one of Cupid’s arrows, Apollo chased the nymph Daphne, who prayed to escape and was turned into a laurel tree. Bernini catches the exact moment of change: bark climbs her legs, leaves burst from her fingers, and her toes root into the ground as Apollo reaches her. Apollo made the laurel his sacred plant, which is why winners and poets were crowned with laurel, and why we still say “laureate.” The Death of HyacinthusGiovanni Battista Tiepolo · 1752–53Apollo loved the Spartan youth Hyacinthus and accidentally killed him with a thrown discus; from his blood grew the hyacinth flower. Tiepolo updates the story by replacing the discus with a tennis ball and racket at the youth’s feet. Apollo’s loves almost always end in loss: Cassandra rejects him, Coronis betrays him, Daphne flees. The Flaying of MarsyasTitian · ca. 1570–76The satyr Marsyas dared to challenge Apollo to a music contest, his pipes against the god’s lyre. He lost, and Apollo had him hung upside down and skinned alive. Titian’s loose, smeared brushwork makes the scene even more disturbing, while King Midas sits in thought nearby. The story warns against hubris, the human or near-human pride that challenges a god.

Theme

Apollo: order and its price

The god of reason and harmony can also be the cruelest of the gods.

Apollo stands for light, music, prophecy, and the rule of measure, the values written on his temple. Yet his myths are full of violence: he kills the Python, flays Marsyas, and sends plague on those who offend him. For the Greeks, order and danger came from the same god, and respecting the limits he set was a matter of survival.

  • Light, music, prophecy, healing
  • Also plague and punishment
  • Know your limits: the Delphic lesson
