The Trojan War

The House of Atreus, Achilles, and the fall of Troy

The Trojan War was the defining legend of the Greek heroic age. It began with a golden apple and a beauty contest, drew every great Greek hero to the walls of Troy for ten years, and ended with the city’s destruction and the long, deadly homecomings of the victors. Homer’s Iliad tells only a few weeks of the tenth year, centered on the anger of Achilles.

“Sing, O goddess, the anger of Achilles son of Peleus, that brought countless ills upon the Achaeans.”

Homer, Iliad 1.1–2, trans. Samuel Butler (1898)
  • Cause: the Judgment of Paris and Helen
  • Ten-year siege; Iliad covers weeks of year ten
  • Fall of Troy and the troubled homecomings
Homer, Iliad
Epic poem of the quarrel between Achilles and Agamemnon and its deadly results.
House of Atreus
The cursed family of Tantalus, Pelops, Atreus, Agamemnon, and Orestes.
Tantalus and Pelops
Tantalus served his son Pelops to the gods; restored to life, Pelops gave his name to the Peloponnese.
Oenomaus, Hippodamia, Myrtilos
Pelops won Hippodamia by bribing the charioteer Myrtilos to sabotage her father’s chariot, then killed him, bringing a curse on his line.
Atreus and Thyestes
Brothers whose feud led Atreus to serve Thyestes his own sons; Thyestes’ son Aegisthus later took revenge.

The François VaseKleitias (painter) and Ergotimos (potter) · ca. 570–565 BCEThe main band shows the gods arriving at the wedding of the mortal Peleus and the sea goddess Thetis, whose son would be Achilles. At that wedding Eris, goddess of strife, who had not been invited, threw in a golden apple marked “for the fairest.” Other bands show Achilles’ later deeds and Ajax carrying his body. Because so many figures are labeled, the vase is a key document of how Greeks told these stories. The Judgement of ParisPeter Paul Rubens · ca. 1632–35Zeus refused to decide which goddess deserved Eris’s apple and sent Hermes to ask the Trojan prince Paris, a son of King Priam and Hecuba, who was tending sheep on Mount Ida. Hera offered power, Athena victory in war, and Aphrodite the most beautiful woman in the world. Paris chose Aphrodite, and his prize was Helen, the wife of Menelaus of Sparta. Rubens shows Paris holding the apple as the three goddesses pose before him.

Theme

A war that began with a choice

One moment of vanity and desire sets the whole heroic world at war.

The golden apple, the beauty contest, and Helen’s departure are a chain of choices that commit gods and men to ten years of slaughter. The oath Helen’s suitors swore turns a private wrong into a war of all Greece. Later artists painted the Judgment of Paris more than almost any other myth, often as an excuse to show three nudes.

  • Eris’s apple, Paris’s choice
  • Suitors’ oath makes it a Greek war
  • Favorite subject of later painters

The Sacrifice of IphigeniaRoman, 1st century CEThe Greek fleet gathered at Aulis, but Artemis held back the winds. The seer Calchas declared that Agamemnon, the commander, must sacrifice his daughter Iphigenia. In the painting she is carried to the altar while Calchas raises a knife and Agamemnon, at left, covers his face; above, Artemis appears with the deer that, in some versions, replaced the girl at the last moment. Amphora with Achilles and Ajax playing a gameExekias · Athenian, ca. 540–530 BCEAchilles, raised by the wise centaur Chiron, was the greatest Greek warrior; his cousin Ajax was the next. Exekias imagines them in a quiet moment, leaning over a board game during a lull in the fighting, spears on their shoulders. Inscriptions give their throws, and Achilles, still wearing his helmet, is winning. The calm makes the violence everyone knows is coming more powerful. Briseis Led Away from AchillesRoman, 1st century CEThe Iliad opens in the tenth year. Agamemnon had taken Chryseis, daughter of Apollo’s priest Chryses, and when Apollo sent a plague he had to return her. To make up his loss he took Achilles’ captive Briseis. Achilles, insulted, withdrew from the fighting and asked his mother Thetis to make the Greeks suffer. Here Patroclus leads Briseis away while Achilles sits in angry dignity. Krater with the Death of SarpedonEuphronios · Athenian, ca. 515 BCEWith Achilles out of the fight, the Trojans drove the Greeks back to their ships. Patroclus put on Achilles’ armor and led a counterattack, killing Sarpedon, a son of Zeus who fought for Troy. Zeus allowed his son’s death but sent Sleep and Death to carry his body home. Euphronios shows the two winged brothers lifting the bleeding hero while Hermes watches. Andromache Mourning HectorJacques-Louis David · 1783Hector, Troy’s greatest defender, killed Patroclus. Grief-stricken, Achilles returned to battle in new armor made by Hephaestus, killed Hector, and dragged his body behind his chariot. Hector’s wife Andromache and their little son Astyanax lost everything. David shows Andromache beside Hector’s body, one hand reaching toward him and the other holding her child. Priam Begging Achilles for the Body of HectorAlexander Ivanov · 1824In the Iliad’s final book, old King Priam, guided by Hermes, crosses the Greek camp at night to beg Achilles for his son’s body. He kisses the hands that killed Hector and asks Achilles to remember his own father. The two enemies weep together, and Achilles returns the body. The poem ends with Hector’s funeral, not with the fall of Troy.

Theme

Glory and its cost

Achilles chooses a short, glorious life over a long, forgotten one.

Achilles knew he could live long in obscurity or die young with undying fame, and he chose fame. The Iliad honors that choice but never hides its price: the deaths of Patroclus, Sarpedon, and Hector, and the grief of fathers, wives, and children. Its last scene, Priam and Achilles weeping together, makes shared mortality the poem’s real subject.

  • Kleos (glory) vs. a long life
  • War’s cost to families
  • Shared grief ends the Iliad
Kleos
Glory or fame won by great deeds, the only immortality a Homeric hero could hope for.

Krater with the Suicide of AjaxEtruscan, ca. 400–350 BCEAfter Achilles was killed by an arrow from Paris, guided by Apollo, the Greeks awarded his armor to Odysseus instead of Ajax. Humiliated, Ajax went mad, then killed himself by falling on his sword. Earlier, Achilles had killed the Amazon queen Penthesilea and fallen in love with her as she died, and mocked by the soldier Thersites, killed him too. The painting shows Ajax kneeling over the sword fixed in the ground. Laocoön and His SonsHagesandros, Athanodoros, and Polydoros of Rhodes · ca. 1st century BCE–1st century CETo take Troy the Greeks needed Achilles’ son Neoptolemus, the bow of Heracles, and the stolen statue of Athena called the Palladium. Then they built a wooden horse, hid soldiers inside, and pretended to sail away. The Trojan priest Laocoön warned against it and threw a spear at it; two giant serpents came from the sea and crushed him and his sons. In Virgil, Trojans took this as a sign and dragged the horse inside. The Procession of the Trojan Horse into TroyGiovanni Domenico Tiepolo · ca. 1760The Trojans pulled the horse into the city and celebrated. That night the Greeks climbed out, opened the gates, and Troy was sacked. Priam was killed at an altar, Astyanax thrown from the walls, Polyxena sacrificed at Achilles’ tomb, and Cassandra, Apollo’s disbelieved prophetess, dragged from Athena’s shrine. Euripides’ Trojan Women shows the captured queens waiting to be shipped away as slaves. Orestes Pursued by the FuriesWilliam-Adolphe Bouguereau · 1862The returns home, or nostoi, were often deadly. Agamemnon came back to Mycenae with Cassandra and was murdered in his bath by Clytemnestra and her lover Aegisthus. Years later his son Orestes, urged by his sister Electra and Apollo, killed his mother and Aegisthus. The Furies, avengers of family blood, hounded him until Athena’s court in Athens acquitted him. Bouguereau shows Orestes covering his ears as the Furies point at his dead mother.
