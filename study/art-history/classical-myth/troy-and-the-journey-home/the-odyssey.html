Troy and the Journey Home

The Odyssey

Odysseus’s ten-year journey home

Homer’s Odyssey follows Odysseus, king of Ithaca, on his ten-year voyage home after Troy, and his wife Penelope and son Telemachus, who wait for him while suitors take over his palace. Where the Iliad celebrates strength, the Odyssey celebrates intelligence, endurance, and the right treatment of guests. Odysseus survives monsters, witches, and goddesses by his wits, then wins back his home in disguise.

“Tell me, O Muse, of that ingenious hero who travelled far and wide after he had sacked the famous town of Troy.”

Homer, Odyssey 1.1–2, trans. Samuel Butler (1900)

Next the cannibal Laestrygonians destroyed every ship but Odysseus’s own. The painter sets the tiny figures of giants hurling rocks in a vast, airy landscape, one of the earliest surviving landscape paintings in Western art. Waterhouse puts the viewer in Odysseus’s place: Circe holds out the cup and wand, and Odysseus appears only as a reflection in the mirror behind her throne. Stamnos with Odysseus and the SirensSiren Painter · Athenian, ca. 480–470 BCEAfter visiting the underworld, Odysseus sailed past the Sirens, whose song lured sailors to their deaths. Odysseus and CalypsoArnold Böcklin · 1882–83Odysseus was washed ashore on Ogygia, island of the nymph Calypso, who kept him for seven years and offered to make him immortal. Theme

Wits over strength

Odysseus survives by cleverness, patience, and disguise.

Odysseus is “much-turning,” a hero of tricks and lies as well as courage. He wins with false names, wax, ropes, and patience, and he loses men whenever they lack self-control. The poem also judges every host: monsters like Polyphemus eat their guests, while the good Phaeacians give gifts. The rules of guest-friendship are the moral backbone of the Odyssey.

  • Tricks: “Nobody,” the mast, disguises
  • Self-control saves, appetite destroys
  • Hosts judged by how they treat guests

Penelope with the SuitorsPinturicchio · ca. 1509At home, more than a hundred suitors had moved into Odysseus’s palace, eating his wealth and pressing Penelope to remarry. She promised to choose once she finished weaving a shroud for her father-in-law Laertes, but unraveled the work every night. Odysseus, disguised as a beggar and helped by his loyal swineherd Eumaeus and his son Telemachus, strung his great bow, which no suitor could bend, and killed them all. Pinturicchio shows Penelope at her loom as the suitors and the disguised Odysseus enter.

Theme

The homecoming

The Odyssey ends not in battle but in recognition.

Odysseus must be recognized by his old dog, his nurse, his son, his wife, and finally his father before he is truly home. Penelope tests him with the secret of their bed, built around a living olive tree. Her loyalty and cleverness match his, and later writers treated her as the model of a faithful wife, in contrast to Clytemnestra.
