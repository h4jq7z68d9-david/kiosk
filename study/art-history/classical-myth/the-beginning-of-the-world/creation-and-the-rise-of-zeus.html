The Beginning of the World

Creation and the Rise of Zeus

From Chaos to the rule of the Olympians

Hesiod’s Theogony (“birth of the gods”) tells how the world began with Chaos and how three generations of gods fought for control of it. Sky-god Uranus is overthrown by his son Cronus, and Cronus by his son Zeus. Zeus then has to defeat the Titans, the monster Typhon, and the Giants before the Olympians rule for good and divide the world among themselves.

“Verily at the first Chaos came to be, but next wide-bosomed Earth”

Hesiod, Theogony 116–117, trans. Hugh G. Evelyn-White (1914)
  • Chaos → Earth → Sky → Titans → Olympians
  • Three cosmic wars secure Zeus’s rule
  • World divided: sky, sea, underworld
Chaos
The first thing to exist in Hesiod: a yawning gap or void, not disorder.
Gaea (Gaia)
Earth, who comes from Chaos and gives birth to the Sky and the Sea, then mother of the Titans and many monsters.
Uranus
The Sky, son and husband of Gaea; overthrown by his son Cronus.
Tartarus
The deepest pit beneath the earth, where defeated gods and monsters are locked away.

The Mutilation of Uranus by SaturnGiorgio Vasari and Cristofano Gherardi · ca. 1556Uranus hated his children and kept them hidden inside Earth, so Gaea made a great sickle and her youngest Titan son, Cronus, ambushed and castrated his father. Blood falling on the earth produced the Furies and the Giants, and the severed parts thrown into the sea produced Aphrodite. Vasari’s ceiling turns this violent episode into the origin of the elements. The Birth of VenusAlexandre Cabanel · 1875In Hesiod’s account, Aphrodite is born from the foam that gathered around Uranus’s severed genitals, which is how ancient writers explained her name (aphros means “foam”). Cabanel turns the story into a dreamy academic nude floating on the waves with cupids overhead. The original version was bought by Napoleon III, showing how mythology gave respectable cover to the nude in 19th-century art. The Fall of PhaetonPeter Paul Rubens · ca. 1604–08The Titan Hyperion fathered the sun (Helios), the moon (Selene), and the dawn (Eos). Helios’s son Phaeton begged to drive the sun’s chariot for one day, lost control, and scorched the earth until Zeus struck him down with a thunderbolt. Ancient writers used the story to explain deserts and why some peoples had dark skin. Rubens shows the moment the horses scatter and the hours of the day flee in panic. SaturnPeter Paul Rubens · 1636–38Cronus, the Roman Saturn, learned that one of his children would overthrow him, so he swallowed each one at birth. His wife Rhea saved the youngest, Zeus, by hiding him on Crete and giving Cronus a stone wrapped in swaddling clothes. Rubens paints an old man biting into a child’s chest, with the stars of the planet Saturn above. Renaissance writers confused Cronus with Chronos, “Time,” which is why he often carries a scythe and appears as Father Time.

Theme

Sons overthrow fathers

Power passes by violence from one generation to the next.

Uranus suppresses his children and is overthrown by Cronus; Cronus swallows his children and is overthrown by Zeus. Zeus breaks the cycle by swallowing his first wife Metis before she can bear a son greater than he is, and Athena is born from his head instead. Goya’s later painting of Saturn, made on the wall of his own house, shows how this ancient story could become an image of pure terror.

  • Uranus → Cronus → Zeus
  • Each father fears a son
  • Zeus ends the cycle by swallowing Metis
Metis
Titaness of cleverness, Zeus’s first wife, swallowed by him while pregnant with Athena.

The Fall of the TitansCornelis van Haarlem · 1588–90The Titanomachy was a ten-year war between the Titans and Zeus’s Olympians. Zeus won only after freeing the Cyclopes, who forged his thunderbolt, and the Hundred-Handers, who hurled boulders. The defeated Titans were locked in Tartarus, and Atlas was condemned to hold up the sky. Van Haarlem shows the Titans tumbling down in a tangle of bodies. Farnese AtlasRoman, 2nd century CE, copy of an earlier originalAtlas, a Titan who fought against Zeus, was punished by being made to hold up the heavens at the western edge of the world. The sculptor shows him bent under a globe carved with the constellations, one of the oldest surviving images of the star groups. His name lives on in the word for a book of maps. Chimera of ArezzoEtruscan, ca. 400 BCEGaea also mated with Pontus, the sea, and her descendants include a line of monsters. The Chimera breathed fire and had a lion’s body, a goat’s head rising from its back, and a snake for a tail; the hero Bellerophon killed it while riding the winged horse Pegasus. This bronze shows the wounded beast snarling, and the goat’s head is already bleeding.

Theme

Monsters with borrowed bodies

Greek monsters mix animal and human parts, an idea borrowed from older art.

Egyptian and Near Eastern art had long combined human and animal parts, often for protective gods. Greek artists borrowed these hybrids, like the sphinx, the griffin, and the winged Harpies, but usually made them threats to be destroyed. Killing a monster became the defining act of a hero, and the monsters stand for the wild disorder that civilization must overcome.

  • Hybrids borrowed from Egypt and the Near East
  • Protective images become threats
  • Heroes prove themselves by killing them

Amphora with the birth of AthenaAthenian, ca. 550–525 BCEZeus swallowed his pregnant wife Metis, and later, suffering a terrible headache, had his head split open with an axe, usually by Hephaestus. Out sprang Athena, fully grown and armed. The painter shows the tiny armored goddess emerging above the seated Zeus. Black-figure painters painted figures in black slip and scratched in details, leaving the red clay as background. Hydria with Zeus fighting TyphonChalcidian, ca. 540–530 BCEAngry at the defeat of her Titan children, Gaea mated with Tartarus and gave birth to Typhon, a winged giant with a hundred snake heads. In the Typhonomachy Zeus fought him with thunderbolts and finally buried him under Mount Etna in Sicily, whose eruptions were said to be the monster’s fire. Here Zeus strides forward with a thunderbolt at the winged, snake-legged Typhon. Pergamon Altar: Athena fighting the Giant AlcyoneusHellenistic, ca. 180–160 BCEThe Gigantomachy was the last great battle, in which the Olympians, with the help of the mortal hero Heracles, defeated the Giants. On this frieze Athena grabs the winged Giant Alcyoneus by the hair, while his mother Gaea rises from the ground pleading for him and Victory flies in to crown the goddess. The writhing, deeply carved bodies are typical of Hellenistic drama. Sala dei Giganti (Room of the Giants)Giulio Romano · 1532–35Giulio Romano painted the Giants crushed under collapsing columns and mountains as Jupiter hurls thunderbolts from the dome above. Because the painting covers the whole room, visitors feel the building falling around them. The theme flattered Federico’s patron, the Emperor Charles V, as a Jupiter who crushes rebels.

Theme

Order defeats chaos

Each cosmic war replaces raw force with Zeus’s order.

The Titans, Typhon, and the Giants are all children of Earth who stand for wild, unchecked power. Zeus wins by alliances and intelligence as much as by force, then shares out the world: he takes the sky, Poseidon the sea, and Hades the underworld. Later rulers, from the kings of Pergamon to Renaissance princes, used these battles to cast their own enemies as monsters.

  • Three wars: Titans, Typhon, Giants
  • Zeus shares the world by lot
  • Reused as political propaganda
Division of the world
After victory, Zeus, Poseidon, and Hades cast lots: Zeus took the sky, Poseidon the sea, Hades the underworld; earth and Olympus were shared.

Creation and the Rise of Zeus

Another way to begin

A creation story from a cosmic egg

Hesiod’s was not the only Greek creation story. A version preserved in Aristophanes’ comedy Birds (414 BCE) and in later Orphic poems begins with Night (Nyx), pictured as a black-winged bird, who lays an egg in the darkness. From it hatches Eros, the force of love, who brings the other elements together; Homer, meanwhile, calls Oceanus the origin of the gods.

  • Night lays a cosmic egg
  • Eros hatches first and joins things together
  • Several Greek origin stories existed side by side
Nyx
Night, one of the first beings from Chaos; in the egg story, the black-winged mother of Eros.
Eros
Desire, one of the oldest powers in Hesiod and the first-born in the egg story; later the boy Cupid.
Oceanus
Titan of the great river encircling the earth, called the origin of the gods in Homer’s Iliad.
