The Beginning of the World

The Origins of Mortals

Prometheus, Pandora, the Five Ages, and the Flood

The Greeks had no single story of how humans were made; Hesiod never says. Later writers credit the Titan Prometheus with shaping people from clay, and every version agrees that he became humankind’s champion against Zeus. His tricks cost humans dearly: Zeus answered with Pandora, the first woman, and the stories go on to explain toil, sickness, marriage, and a long decline from a golden age.

  • Prometheus shapes and protects humans
  • Zeus’s answer: Pandora and her jar
  • Decline through five ages; the flood
Prometheus
Titan whose name means “forethought”; trickster who sides with humans, steals fire, and is punished by Zeus.
Epimetheus
Prometheus’s brother, “afterthought,” who accepts Pandora despite being warned against gifts from Zeus.

The Myth of PrometheusPiero di Cosimo · ca. 1510–15Piero tells the story as a sequence: Prometheus shapes a clay statue of a man, flies up with Athena to light a torch from the sun’s chariot, and brings the fire down to give the figure life. In this version, drawn from later writers, Prometheus is less a trickster than a creator, an idea Renaissance thinkers compared to God shaping Adam. Sarcophagus with Prometheus creating humankindRoman, ca. 240 CEPrometheus sits modeling a small human figure while gods watch, among them Athena in her helmet and Hermes in his winged hat. In the Roman versions known from Ovid and later writers, Prometheus mixes earth and water to make people in the gods’ image, and Athena gives them reason. Christian writers later saw a pagan parallel to God forming Adam from earth in Genesis. Cup with Atlas and PrometheusArkesilas Painter · Laconian (Spartan), ca. 560–550 BCETwo brothers suffer side by side: Atlas holds up the sky, and Prometheus, bound to a post, is attacked by Zeus’s eagle, which eats his liver every day while it grows back each night. This is one of the earliest images of the myth. In Hesiod the hero Heracles later kills the eagle and frees Prometheus, with Zeus’s consent. Prometheus Carrying FireJan Cossiers, after a sketch by Rubens · 1636–38At a sacrifice at Mecone, Prometheus hid the good meat of an ox inside its unappetizing stomach and wrapped the bones in shining fat, and Zeus chose the fat. That trick explained why Greeks burned bones and fat for the gods and ate the meat themselves. Zeus retaliated by hiding fire, and Prometheus stole it back in a hollow fennel stalk, the burning torch he runs with here. Prometheus Being Chained by VulcanDirck van Baburen · 1623Vulcan, the smith, hammers chains onto Prometheus while Mercury points to the punishment. Baburen borrows Caravaggio’s strong light and dark and his plain, physical bodies, so the scene feels like a real execution. By this time Vulcan stood for technology itself, and Mercury for cunning, the same qualities that got Prometheus into trouble. Prometheus BoundPeter Paul Rubens, eagle by Frans Snyders · 1611–18Rubens shows Prometheus falling headfirst down the rocks as the eagle tears at him, a pose built from his study of ancient sculpture and Michelangelo. In Aeschylus’s play Prometheus Bound, the Titan defends himself by listing what he taught humans: building, counting, writing, medicine, and more. That made him a hero of human progress for later artists and writers.

Theme

The trickster who made us human

Every gift from Prometheus comes with a cost to humans.

Prometheus’s trick at the sacrifice gave humans the meat but left the gods the smoke, setting people halfway between gods and animals. Stealing fire gave them technology but brought on Zeus’s anger and Pandora. The story explains why human life mixes progress with suffering, and later ages turned Prometheus into a symbol of creativity, rebellion, and the price of knowledge.

  • Sacrifice places humans between gods and beasts
  • Fire = technology and punishment
  • Later symbol of creativity and rebellion

Epimetheus Opening Pandora’s VaseGiulio Bonasone · mid-16th centuryTo punish humans for the stolen fire, Zeus had Hephaestus shape the first woman from earth and water; Athena taught her weaving, Aphrodite gave her desire, and Hermes gave her a deceitful mind. She was called Pandora, “all gifts,” and was presented to Epimetheus. Bonasone’s print is unusual in showing Epimetheus himself lifting the lid as the evils fly out. Eva Prima PandoraJean Cousin the Elder · ca. 1550Cousin merges two first women: Eve of Genesis and Pandora of Greek myth. She reclines with a skull under her arm, an apple branch, and a jar, while a snake curls around her arm. Both stories blame a woman’s curiosity for bringing suffering into the world, and Renaissance thinkers liked to treat pagan myths as echoes of the Bible. PandoraJohn William Waterhouse · 1896Waterhouse shows Pandora kneeling in a wood, peering into a golden box as a wisp of smoke escapes. In Hesiod the container is a large storage jar (pithos); it became a “box” through a Renaissance mistranslation, and later versions add the rule that she must not open it. When she lifts the lid, sickness and toil escape, and only Hope stays inside. The DanaïdesJohn William Waterhouse · 1903The fifty daughters of Danaus were forced to marry their cousins, and all but one killed her husband on the wedding night. In the underworld they were sentenced to carry water forever in leaking jars that never fill. Waterhouse paints them pouring endlessly into a basin. The jar, like Pandora’s, links women with containers, and Greek ideas of marriage cast the bride as a vessel for children.

Theme

Woman as a container

Greek myth repeatedly pictures women as vessels.

Pandora comes with a jar, the Danaïds carry broken jars, and Greek women in daily life carried water jars and wedding gifts. Writers compared a woman to a jar or a stomach: something that holds and consumes. Hesiod treats women as a costly necessity, since a man without children has no one to care for him in old age. These stories say more about Greek men’s anxieties than about real women.

  • Jar = womb, storage, and burial urn
  • Marriage resented but necessary
  • Myth reflects male anxieties

The Golden AgeLucas Cranach the Elder · ca. 1530Hesiod describes five races of humans, each worse than the last. In the Golden Age under Cronus, people lived like gods, without work, illness, or old age, and died as if falling asleep. Cranach paints them dancing and bathing in a walled garden, an image close to the Christian Eden. The silver race was foolish, the bronze violent, and the heroes better, but Hesiod’s own Iron Age is the worst of all. The Flood Tablet (Epic of Gilgamesh, Tablet XI)Neo-Assyrian, 7th century BCEWhen George Smith of the British Museum translated this tablet in 1872, he found a flood story strikingly like Noah’s: the gods decide to destroy humanity, one god warns a good man to build a boat, and he releases birds to find land. Older Sumerian and Akkadian versions name the hero Ziusudra and Atrahasis. The Greek flood story belongs to the same family. The DelugeMichelangelo · 1508–09Michelangelo shows desperate families climbing to high ground while Noah’s ark floats in the distance. Placed among Greek myths, the scene shows how widely the flood story spread. Each culture gives it a different reason: overpopulation in Mesopotamia, human wickedness in Genesis and in the Greek version. Deucalion and PyrrhaPeter Paul Rubens · 1636–37In the Greek version, told most fully by Ovid, Zeus floods the world after the king Lycaon serves him human flesh. Only the good couple Deucalion, son of Prometheus, and Pyrrha, daughter of Epimetheus and Pandora, survive in a chest or boat. Told by an oracle to throw “the bones of their mother” behind them, they toss stones, the bones of Mother Earth, which become new men and women.

Theme

A world in decline

Greek myth tells human history as a fall, not as progress.

Hesiod’s ages run downhill from gold to iron, and the flood wipes the slate almost clean. Humans fall from a god-like life because of Prometheus’s tricks and Pandora’s jar, much as Adam and Eve are expelled from Eden. Later, the Roman poet Virgil imagined the ages repeating in a cycle, so that Augustus could announce the return of a Golden Age.

  • History as decline: gold to iron
  • Flood as divine reset
  • Rome promised a new Golden Age
