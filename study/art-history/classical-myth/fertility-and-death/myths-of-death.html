Fertility and Death

Myths of Death

The underworld and its visitors

For most Greeks, the dead became weak, shadowy images of themselves in the gloomy realm of Hades, whatever kind of life they had lived. Only a few great sinners were singled out for punishment and a few favored heroes for paradise. Over the centuries, mystery cults, philosophers, and Roman poets turned this shadowland into a place of judgment and reward, a picture that passed into Christian ideas of hell and heaven.

“The gates of hell are open night and day; Smooth the descent, and easy is the way”

Virgil, Aeneid 6, trans. John Dryden (1697)
  • Hades: shadowy afterlife for most
  • Punishment for a few, paradise for fewer
  • Later writers add judgment and reward
Soul (psyche, anima)
The breath or life-spirit that leaves the body at death; psyche in Greek, anima in Latin.
Hades
“The unseen one,” the god and his realm; the Romans called the god Pluto, “the enricher.”
Orcus
A Roman name for the underworld and its god, “the place that confines.”
Eidolon
The image or shade of a dead person, a thin copy of the living.
Anthesteria
Athenian spring festival of new wine when the spirits of the dead were believed to roam, sometimes compared to Halloween.

Families placed an obol in the mouth of the dead for the fare. White-ground lekythoi, painted in delicate colors on a chalky white surface, show these last journeys and quiet farewells at the tomb. The small soul in the boat looks toward hell. The painting mixes the classical ferryman with the Christian choice between salvation and damnation. Relief with Odysseus consulting TiresiasRoman, late 1st century CEIn Odyssey 11, the witch Circe sends Odysseus to the edge of the world, the land of the Cimmerians, to consult the dead seer Tiresias. Odysseus’s visit is an act of necromancy, calling up the dead to learn the future.

Theme

A gloomy afterlife for almost everyone

Homer’s dead envy the living.

In the Odyssey, the shades Odysseus meets teach hard lessons. Agamemnon, murdered by his wife, warns against trusting women; Achilles says he would rather be a poor laborer on earth than king of all the dead; Ajax, still angry over a lost contest, refuses to speak. King Minos judges disputes, and Orion still hunts. Fame on earth was the only real immortality.

  • Agamemnon: betrayal
  • Achilles: life above all
  • Ajax: anger outlasts death
Lessons of the dead
Agamemnon, Achilles, Ajax, King Minos, and Orion appear in Odyssey 11, each showing what death does to a hero.

SisyphusTitian · 1548–49A few sinners earned eternal punishment in Tartarus. Sisyphus, who cheated death, pushes a boulder uphill forever; Tantalus stands in water that recedes when he bends to drink, beneath fruit he cannot reach; Ixion, who tried to seduce Hera, spins on a fiery wheel; and Tityus has his liver eaten by vultures. Titian’s Sisyphus bends under a rock that seems to crush him.

Theme

Punishments that fit the crime

The great sinners all tried to take what belonged to the gods.

Each famous sinner offended the gods directly: Tantalus tested them by serving his son as a meal, Ixion lusted after Hera, Sisyphus tricked Death itself. Their punishments are endless repetitions or frustrations, making the crime visible forever. The idea of matching punishment to sin lasted into Dante’s Inferno.

  • Sinners offended the gods themselves
  • Endless, repeated torments
  • Model for Dante’s hell

Orpheus, Eurydice, and HermesRoman copy, 1st century BCE, of an Athenian relief of ca. 410 BCEThe poet Orpheus, whose music could move stones, went down to Hades to bring back his wife Eurydice, who had died from a snakebite. Charmed by his song, the gods let her go on one condition: he must not look back. He turned at the last moment, and she was lost. The relief shows the farewell, as Hermes gently takes her wrist to lead her back. Orpheus Leading Eurydice from the UnderworldJean-Baptiste-Camille Corot · 1861Corot imagines the underworld as a misty, silvery grove, closer to the Elysian Fields than to a place of torment. Orpheus raises his lyre and leads Eurydice by the hand, not yet looking back. In the Greek imagination most souls drifted through dim Asphodel Meadows, while a lucky few heroes went to Elysium, a paradise of endless calm. The Map of HellSandro Botticelli · ca. 1480–95Plato’s Republic ends with the Myth of Er, a soldier who returns from death to describe souls being judged, rewarded, punished, and choosing new lives. Ideas like these flowed into Christian visions of the afterlife. Dante’s Inferno arranges hell as a funnel of nine circles, staffed by classical figures such as Charon, Minos, and Cerberus, which Botticelli maps from top to bottom. Aeneas and the Sibyl in the UnderworldJan Brueghel the Younger · 1630sIn Book 6 of Virgil’s Aeneid, the Cumaean Sibyl leads Aeneas down through a cave near Lake Avernus to visit his dead father, Anchises. They cross the Acheron in Charon’s boat and pass the places of punishment before reaching Elysium, where Anchises shows his son the souls of future Romans. Brueghel crowds the dark landscape with fires, monsters, and tiny tormented figures.

Theme

From shadows to judgment

The afterlife becomes moral over time.

Homer’s underworld is mostly the same for everyone. Mystery cults promised initiates a better fate, Orphics and Plato added judgment and reincarnation, and Virgil arranged the dead by their deeds and gave Rome a destiny. Dante then built hell, purgatory, and heaven from these classical pieces, and artists kept painting the result for centuries.

  • Homer: same fate for nearly all
  • Mysteries, Orphics, Plato: reward and rebirth
  • Virgil and Dante: an ordered moral world
