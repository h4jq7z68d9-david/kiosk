Myth and Its World

The Cultural Context

The Greek world that produced the myths

Greek myths took shape over two thousand years of history, from the Bronze Age palaces of Crete and Mycenae to the Hellenistic kingdoms that followed Alexander the Great. A rocky, mountainous land pushed the Greeks toward the sea, trade, and independent city-states. The myths also carry older ideas from Mesopotamia, and they were first written down in the Archaic period by poets who looked back on a lost heroic age.

  • Historical periods: Minoan → Mycenaean → Dark Age → Archaic → Classical → Hellenistic
  • Mountains and sea → trade, colonies, separate city-states
  • Near Eastern roots; written down ca. 700 BCE
Greek historical periods
Early/Middle Bronze Age (Minoan) 3000–1600 BCE; Late Bronze Age (Mycenaean) 1600–1150; Dark Age 1150–800; Archaic 800–480; Classical 480–323; Hellenistic 323–31 BCE.
Greek art periods
Geometric 900–700 BCE; Orientalizing 700–600; Archaic 600–480; Early Classical 480–450; High Classical 450–400; Late Classical 400–323; Hellenistic 323–31 BCE.
Indo-Europeans
Peoples speaking related languages, Greek among them, who spread from Central Asia across Europe; their societies were ranked into rulers and priests, warriors, and food producers.

The maze of rooms at Knossos may lie behind the myth of the Labyrinth, and the bull images that decorated it echo the story of the Minotaur. Heinrich Schliemann, who excavated here in the 1870s, believed he had found the world of Homer’s king Agamemnon, and this tomb still carries the name of Agamemnon’s father. The Mycenaeans kept palace accounts in Linear B, an early form of Greek, but did not use writing for stories.

Bronze Age ruins behind the myths

Heroic myths remember, loosely, a real world that had vanished.

Around 1150 BCE the Mycenaean palaces collapsed, writing disappeared, and Greece entered a Dark Age. When poets later told stories of Minos, Agamemnon, and Troy, they were describing a grand past they knew only from ruins and inherited songs. Archaeology has since shown that places like Knossos, Mycenae, and Troy were real, even if the stories told about them are not history.

  • Palace collapse ca. 1150 BCE
  • Dark Age: loss of writing
  • Myths set in real, ruined places
Dark Age
The period after the Mycenaean collapse (ca. 1150–800 BCE) marked by depopulation and the loss of writing.

Myth in a man’s world

Most of what survives was written by and for elite men.

Almost all of our sources come from aristocratic men in Athens in the 400s and 300s BCE. Myths were performed at their symposia, paid for by their families, and shaped by their views of war, honor, and women. Reading the myths with this in mind helps explain why heroes are male warriors and why women in the stories are so often dangerous, silent, or blamed.

  • Sources: elite Athenian men
  • Performed at symposia and festivals
  • Shapes how women appear in myth

That chain of borrowing is how Near Eastern stories reached the Greeks.

Myth’s roots in the Near East

Greek myth grew out of much older Mesopotamian traditions.

Sumerians, Akkadians, and Babylonians told of gods with human passions, generations of gods overthrowing one another, and a flood sent to destroy humankind. Greek stories borrowed and reshaped these patterns, just as Rome would later borrow from Greece. The Hebrew Bible draws on the same Mesopotamian stock, which is why Genesis and Greek myth sometimes look alike.

  • Sumer → Akkad → Babylon → Greece
  • Shared patterns: divine generations, flood
  • Genesis draws on the same sources

The Cultural Context

Where the stories come from

The main written sources

Greek myths were told for centuries before Homer and Hesiod wrote versions down around 700 BCE. Athenian tragedy reworked them for the stage in the 400s BCE, and Roman poets gave them their most influential forms. The works below are the main sources later artists read.

  • Homer and Hesiod: earliest written versions (ca. 700 BCE)
  • Tragedy: myth staged in Classical Athens
  • Virgil and Ovid: the Roman retellings artists used most
Homer, Iliad and Odyssey
Epic poems; the Iliad covers weeks in the tenth year of the Trojan War, the Odyssey Odysseus’s ten-year voyage home.
Hesiod, Theogony and Works and Days
The Theogony tells the birth of the gods and Zeus’s rise; Works and Days gives advice on life and includes Prometheus, Pandora, and the Five Ages.
Tragedy: Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides
Athenian playwrights who staged myths at festivals of Dionysus; Aeschylus stresses the gods, Sophocles fate, Euripides human passion.
Virgil, Aeneid (29–19 BCE)
Roman epic written under Augustus, tracing Aeneas from fallen Troy to Italy and Rome’s founding.
Ovid, Metamorphoses (8 CE)
A long Roman poem retelling hundreds of myths of transformation; the single most-used source for later artists.
