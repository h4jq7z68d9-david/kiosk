Myth and Its World

The Nature of Myth

What myth is, the kinds of myth, and the Olympian gods

A myth is a traditional story that a whole community treats as important. It has a plot, characters in conflict, and a setting in the distant past, and it was passed along by word of mouth long before anyone wrote it down. Because every teller changed the details, Greek myths never had one official version. This section introduces the main kinds of myth and the twelve Olympian gods who drive most of the stories that later artists painted and carved.

  • Myth = story with a plot, conflict, and a far-off past
  • Three kinds: divine myth, legend, folktale
  • Greek gods kept by Rome under Latin names
Myth
A traditional story with importance for a whole community, not just one teller; the subject of every work on this site.
Mythos
The Greek word behind “myth,” first meaning an authoritative spoken account rather than a made-up tale.
Pantheon
The full set of a culture’s gods taken as a group, such as the Greek Olympians.
Divine myth
A story whose main actors are gods and other supernatural beings, set before human time (for example, the war of gods and Giants).
Legend (saga)
A story about human heroes, often nobles, set in real places such as Troy or Athens; it works like ancient history.
Folktale
A story about ordinary people or talking animals told to entertain and teach social rules, like Aesop’s fables.
Oral tradition
Passing stories along by memory and performance; it explains why the same myth survives in different versions.

Myths explain the world

Many myths were the ancient answer to questions that science answers today.

Why do volcanoes rumble? Because Zeus pinned the monster Typhon under Mount Etna. Why does winter come? Because Hades carried Persephone to the underworld and her mother Demeter grieves while she is gone. Stories like these, which explain how something came to be, are called etiological myths, and they run through almost every story in this course.

  • Etiology = explanation of a cause or origin
  • Seasons: Persephone’s stay with Hades
  • Volcanoes: Typhon trapped under Etna
Etiological tale
A myth that explains the origin of a natural event, custom, or ritual; many divine myths do this.

No single true version

Every poet, playwright, and vase painter retold a myth his own way.

Greek myths circulated orally for centuries before Homer and Hesiod wrote versions down around 700 BCE. Later poets, playwrights, sculptors, and painters changed names, motives, and endings to suit their audiences. There was no sacred book and no church to decide which version was correct, so comparing versions is part of studying myth, and images are often our only source for a version no text preserves.

  • Written down first in the Archaic period (700s BCE)
  • No authoritative text or priesthood
  • Vase paintings preserve their own versions

Jupiter of SmyrnaRoman, 2nd century CEZeus, called Jupiter by the Romans, is the king of the gods and the god of the sky and thunder. He is the youngest son of the Titans Cronus and Rhea and became ruler after overthrowing his father. As judge of oaths and guest-friendship he stands for order and justice, yet the myths also give him more lovers than any other god, which explains the many demigod heroes. The sculptor shows him the standard way: a mature bearded man, bare-chested, with a scepter and thunderbolt. Campana HeraRoman, 2nd century CE, after a Greek modelHera, the Roman Juno, is queen of the gods and goddess of marriage and the family. She is Zeus’s sister and wife, and in myth she punishes his lovers and their children, most famously Heracles, whose name means “glory of Hera.” The statue shows her fully dressed and dignified, the opposite of the nude goddesses of love. Her peacock, a common attribute, carries the hundred eyes of her watchman Argus. Bronze god from Cape ArtemisionGreek, ca. 460 BCEPoseidon, the Roman Neptune, rules the sea, earthquakes, and horses, and he is a brother of Zeus and Hades. This bronze once held a weapon that is now lost, so scholars still debate whether it showed Poseidon with his trident or Zeus with a thunderbolt. Either way it shows the Early Classical ideal: a perfectly balanced, mature body caught at the moment before a throw. Bronzes this size rarely survive, because most were melted down for their metal. Great Eleusinian ReliefGreek, ca. 440–430 BCEDemeter, the Roman Ceres, is the goddess of grain, farming, and the seasons; the word “cereal” comes from her Latin name. Here she hands ears of grain to the young prince Triptolemus, who will teach farming to humankind, while her daughter Persephone, holding a torch, crowns him. The relief stood at Eleusis, where worshippers were initiated into rites believed to give hope for a better afterlife. The Return of PersephoneFrederic Leighton · 1891Persephone, the Roman Proserpina, is Demeter’s daughter and became queen of the underworld after Hades carried her off. Because she had eaten pomegranate seeds there, she must return to him for part of each year, and her absence explains winter. Leighton paints the moment Hermes lifts her out of the dark toward her mother, who reaches up in the sunlight. The story turns a family’s grief into the cycle of the seasons. Mattei AthenaRoman copy of a Greek bronze of the mid-4th century BCEAthena, the Roman Minerva, is the virgin goddess of wisdom, crafts such as weaving, and the planning side of war. She was born fully armed from Zeus’s head after he swallowed her mother, the Titaness Metis (“cleverness”). She wears a helmet and the aegis, a goatskin cape bearing the Gorgon’s head. Athens, named for her, saw her as its protector. Hestia GiustinianiRoman copy after a Greek original of ca. 470–460 BCEHestia, the Roman Vesta, is the goddess of the hearth, the fire at the center of every home and city. She has almost no stories of her own, because her role was simply to be present wherever a fire was kept. In Rome the Vestal Virgins tended the city’s sacred flame. Some later lists of the Twelve Olympians drop Hestia and include Dionysus instead. Bacchus and AriadneTitian · 1520–23Dionysus, the Roman Bacchus, is the god of wine, ecstasy, and theater, and the only Olympian with a mortal mother, the Theban princess Semele. Titian shows him leaping from his leopard-drawn chariot toward Ariadne, the Cretan princess abandoned by Theseus, whom he marries and later places in the sky as a constellation. His noisy followers, the satyrs and maenads, fill the right side. Apollo BelvedereRoman copy, ca. 120–140 CE, after a Greek bronze of ca. 330 BCEApollo, whose name stayed the same in Latin, is the god of light, music, poetry, prophecy, healing, and archery. He is the son of Zeus and Leto and the twin of Artemis. His oracle at Delphi was the most important in Greece. The statue shows him having just shot an arrow, his bow once in the outstretched left hand. Diana of VersaillesRoman copy, 1st–2nd century CE, of a Greek bronze of ca. 325 BCEArtemis, the Roman Diana, is the virgin goddess of the hunt, wild animals, the moon, and childbirth. Twin sister of Apollo, she lives in the wilderness among nymphs. Here she reaches for an arrow from her quiver while a deer runs beside her. Ancient art keeps her clothed; stories warn of what happens to men who see her bathing, like the hunter Actaeon. Ludovisi AresRoman copy of a Greek original of ca. 320 BCEAres, the Roman Mars, is the god of war in its violent, bloody form, unlike Athena, who stands for planning and strategy. The Greeks disliked him, but the Romans honored Mars as the father of Romulus, and the month of March is named for him. Here he sits at rest with his shield, and a small Eros at his feet hints at his love affair with Aphrodite. The Birth of VenusSandro Botticelli · ca. 1484–86Aphrodite, the Roman Venus, is the goddess of love, beauty, and desire. In Hesiod she rises from sea foam after Cronus throws his father Uranus’s severed genitals into the sea, while Homer calls her a daughter of Zeus and Dione. Botticelli shows her arriving on a shell, blown ashore by the winds and met by a goddess of the seasons. She is married to Hephaestus but loves Ares. Apollo in the Forge of VulcanDiego Velázquez · 1630Hephaestus, the Roman Vulcan, is the smith and craftsman of the gods, the maker of their weapons and palaces, and the only Olympian with a physical disability. Velázquez shows Apollo arriving in his forge to tell him that his wife, Aphrodite, is having an affair with Ares, and the workers freeze in shock. The word “volcano” comes from Vulcan, whose forge was imagined beneath the earth. Hermes and the Infant DionysusAttributed to Praxiteles · ca. 340–330 BCE (some scholars date it later)Hermes, the Roman Mercury, is the messenger of the gods, the god of travelers, trade, and thieves, and the guide who leads souls to the underworld. He is the son of Zeus and the nymph Maia. Here he carries his baby half-brother Dionysus to the nymphs who will raise him, safe from Hera’s anger, and once dangled a bunch of grapes in his missing right hand. The relaxed S-curve pose and soft, polished surfaces made Praxiteles the most admired sculptor of the 4th century BCE. Pluto (as Serapis) and Persephone (as Isis) with CerberusRoman, 2nd century CEHades, the Roman Pluto (“the rich one”), rules the underworld and the dead. He is the oldest brother of Zeus and Poseidon but lives below the earth, so he is usually not counted among the Olympians. Here he stands with a scepter beside his three-headed dog Cerberus, next to his queen Persephone. The pair are dressed as the Egyptian gods Serapis and Isis, showing how Roman religion blended Greek and Egyptian gods.

Reading a god by attributes

Artists name a god by objects and animals, not by a face.

Greek and Roman gods look like ideal humans, so a statue of Apollo and one of an athlete can be almost the same body. What tells viewers who is who is the attribute: a thunderbolt for Zeus, a trident for Poseidon, a bow and deer for Artemis, a caduceus for Hermes. Renaissance and later painters kept the same system, so the code still works across two thousand years of art.

  • Gods = ideal human bodies
  • Identity carried by objects and animals
  • Same code used in Renaissance and later art

Why Rome kept the Greek gods

Rome matched its own gods to the Greek ones and gave them Roman jobs.

Rome came into contact with Greek culture through trade and the Greek cities of southern Italy, and it matched its own deities to the Greek ones: Jupiter to Zeus, Juno to Hera, Minerva to Athena. The Romans kept Greek stories and copied Greek statues, as many works on this site show, but reorganized the gods around the needs of the Roman state. The three most important became the Capitoline Triad, worshipped together on the Capitoline Hill.

  • Roman gods matched to Greek counterparts
  • Greek statues copied for Roman settings
  • State religion centered on the Capitoline Triad
Capitoline Triad
Jupiter, Juno, and Minerva, the three chief gods of Roman state religion, honored together in the great temple on Rome’s Capitoline Hill.
