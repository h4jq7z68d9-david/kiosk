Heroes of Athens and Thebes

Thebes, Oedipus, and Jason

A cursed city, a tragic king, and the voyage of the Argo

Thebes was a city founded in blood and haunted by curses, and its royal family produced the most famous tragedy of all: Oedipus, who killed his father and married his mother without knowing it. Jason’s quest for the Golden Fleece, by contrast, is an adventure story, though it too ends in disaster when he betrays the sorceress Medea. Athenian playwrights turned both legends into tragedies still performed today.

  • Thebes: founded by Cadmus, cursed for generations
  • Oedipus: fate, guilt, and knowledge
  • Jason and Medea: quest and betrayal

Two Followers of Cadmus Devoured by a DragonCornelis van Haarlem · 1588Cadmus, searching for his sister Europa, was told by Delphi to found a city where a cow lay down. When his men went for water, a dragon sacred to Ares killed them; Cadmus slew it and, on Athena’s advice, sowed its teeth, from which armed men sprang up and fought until five remained, the Spartoi, ancestors of Thebes’ nobles. Van Haarlem shows the dragon tearing into the companions in the foreground while Cadmus fights in the distance. Cup with Oedipus and the SphinxOedipus Painter · Athenian, ca. 470 BCEAn oracle warned King Laius of Thebes that his son would kill him, so he and Queen Jocasta exposed their baby with his ankles pinned, giving him the name Oedipus, “swollen foot.” A shepherd saved him, and he was raised by King Polybus and Queen Merope of Corinth. Fleeing a prophecy that he would kill his father, he killed a stranger at a crossroads: Laius. At Thebes he answered the Sphinx’s riddle, which this cup shows him pondering. Oedipus and the SphinxJean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres · 1808 (enlarged 1827)Oedipus solved the riddle, the Sphinx killed herself, and grateful Thebes made him king and gave him the widowed queen Jocasta. They had four children: Antigone, Ismene, Polynices, and Eteocles. When plague struck, Oedipus vowed to find Laius’s killer and slowly discovered that it was himself, and that his wife was his mother. Jocasta hanged herself and Oedipus blinded himself. Ingres shows the calm, confident hero before he knows the truth.

Theme

Tragedy: knowledge that destroys

Oedipus’s search for truth is what ruins him.

Aristotle praised Oedipus the King because its recognition and reversal happen at the same moment: the more Oedipus learns, the further he falls. The play raises questions with no easy answer. Was Oedipus guilty if he acted in ignorance? Can anyone escape a prophecy? Tragedy let Athenian audiences feel pity and fear safely, and these legends of Thebes became its richest source.

  • Recognition and reversal together
  • Guilt without intent; fate vs. choice
  • Pity and fear, felt safely in the theater

Antigone Gives Token Burial to the Body of Her Brother PolynicesJules-Eugène Lenepveu · 1835–98After Oedipus left Thebes, his sons agreed to share the throne, but Eteocles refused to give it up. Polynices raised an army, the Seven against Thebes, and the brothers killed each other. Their uncle Creon, now king, forbade burial of the traitor Polynices. Their sister Antigone buried him anyway, choosing the laws of the gods over the king’s command, and was sentenced to be walled up alive. Jason with the Golden FleeceBertel Thorvaldsen · 1802–03 (marble 1828)The Golden Fleece came from a flying ram sent to rescue Phrixus and Helle, children of King Athamas, from their stepmother Ino; Helle fell into the sea (the Hellespont), and Phrixus reached Colchis, where King Aeetes hung the fleece in a grove guarded by a dragon. Jason, whose uncle Pelias had taken the throne of Iolcus, was sent to fetch it. Thorvaldsen shows him striding home with the fleece over his arm, modeled on ancient statues. Medea about to Kill Her ChildrenEugène Delacroix · 1838On the voyage the Argonauts met the women of Lemnos, King Cyzicus, the boxer Amycus, and the blind prophet Phineus, whom the winged brothers Zetes and Calais freed from the Harpies; Heracles was left behind searching for his beloved Hylas. In Colchis, Aeetes’ daughter Medea used her magic to help Jason yoke fire-breathing bulls and take the fleece, and on the way home she brought down the bronze giant Talos. Years later Jason abandoned her for a Corinthian princess, Glauce, and Medea killed the bride and her own children. Delacroix shows her hiding in a cave, dagger in hand, as her pursuers approach.

Theme

The foreign woman

Medea is both the hero’s helper and his ruin.

Like Ariadne for Theseus, Medea betrays her own family to help a Greek hero, and like Ariadne she is abandoned. But Medea fights back. Euripides makes her the most frightening woman on the Greek stage, a foreigner with magic powers who refuses to be discarded. Audiences were invited both to fear her and to feel the injustice done to her.

  • Helper who betrays her family
  • Abandoned by the hero
  • Feared and pitied: Euripides’ Medea

Sarcophagus with the Calydonian Boar HuntRoman, 2nd–3rd century CEArtemis sent a monstrous boar to ravage Calydon, and Prince Meleager gathered heroes, among them the huntress Atalanta, to kill it. Atalanta and HippomenesGuido Reni · ca. 1620–25Atalanta, a swift huntress who wished to stay unmarried, raced every suitor and killed those she beat. Hippomenes (or Melanion) prayed to Aphrodite, who gave him three golden apples, from the Garden of the Hesperides or, in Ovid, from her own sacred grove on Cyprus. During the race he dropped them one by one, and Atalanta stopped to pick them up and lost. Reni turns the race into a slow, elegant dance of two pale bodies.
