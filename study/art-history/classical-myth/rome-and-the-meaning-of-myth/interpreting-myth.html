Rome and the Meaning of Myth

Interpreting Myth

How people have tried to explain what myths mean

Almost as soon as myths were written down, people began asking what they really meant. Greek philosophers read them as hidden science or moral lessons, Christians as pagan echoes of the Bible, and modern scholars as ritual, language, psychology, social structure, or politics. Each theory reveals something, and each age’s favorite theory says as much about that age as about the myths.

  • Ancient: allegory and history
  • Christian and Renaissance: moral and spiritual meanings
  • Modern: ritual, language, mind, structure, context
Allegory
Reading a story as a coded message about something else.
Symbolism
Treating figures and objects in a myth as signs for ideas.

Interpreting Myth

Greek theories

Myth as science, history, and morality

Greek thinkers were the first critics of myth. Some philosophers, offended by stories of gods behaving badly, read the myths as hidden truths about nature or ethics. Others treated them as distorted history.

  • Myths contain hidden philosophical or historical truth
  • Gods as forces of nature or virtues
  • Gods as deified ancient kings
Philosophical or historical truth
The view that myths preserve real knowledge or real events in disguised form.
Physical allegory
Reading gods as natural forces: Zeus as sky, Hera as air, Hephaestus as fire.
Etymology
Explaining a god’s nature from his or her name, a favorite method of Stoic writers.
Euhemerism
The theory of Euhemerus (ca. 300 BCE) that gods were great human kings later worshipped.
Moral allegory
Reading myths as lessons in virtue and vice, such as Heracles at the crossroads.
Ovide moralisé en prose15th centuryMedieval Christians kept reading Ovid by treating his myths as allegories of Christian truth. The long French poem called the Ovide moralisé (about 1300) retells the Metamorphoses and then explains each story: Phaethon’s fall stands for pride, Orpheus can stand for Christ. Prose versions like this one carried the method into the 1400s, and it shaped how Renaissance artists understood classical subjects. PrimaveraSandro Botticelli · ca. 1480Venus stands in her garden with Cupid above, flanked by the Three Graces, Mercury, Flora, and the west wind Zephyr seizing the nymph Chloris. Many scholars read it through the Neoplatonism of the Florentine philosopher Marsilio Ficino, who taught that earthly beauty and love lead the soul upward toward the divine. Whatever its exact meaning, it shows how Renaissance thinkers treated myth as philosophy.

Interpreting Myth

From the Enlightenment to anthropology

Myth as superstition, poetry, and ritual

Enlightenment thinkers saw myths as the mistakes of primitive minds, while Romantics prized them as the poetry of nations. In the 1800s and 1900s, scholars compared myths around the world and linked them to rituals and social customs.

  • Enlightenment: myths as early, mistaken science
  • Romantics: myths as national poetry and imagination
  • Anthropology: myths tied to ritual and society
Enlightenment theories
18th-century views of myth as superstition from humanity’s childhood.
Romantic theories
19th-century views of myth as the creative expression of a people’s spirit.
Ritual theory
The idea that myths grew out of rituals and explain them (James Frazer, Jane Harrison).
Charter theory
Bronisław Malinowski’s idea that myths justify a society’s customs and institutions.
Metope with Helios in his chariotHellenistic, ca. 300–280 BCEThe sun god rises in his four-horse chariot, his head crowned with rays. In the 1800s the philologist Max Müller argued that most myths began as poetic descriptions of the sun, dawn, and sky, misunderstood as language changed, so that a story like Daphne fleeing Apollo meant the dawn fleeing the sun. Solar mythology faded, but comparing myths across Indo-European languages remained influential, notably in Georges Dumézil’s theory of three social functions. Oedipus and the SphinxGustave Moreau · 1864Moreau’s Sphinx clings to Oedipus’s chest, a beautiful and deadly woman locked in a staring match with the hero, above the remains of earlier victims. Within a few decades Sigmund Freud would read the Oedipus myth as a key to the mind, and Carl Jung would see myths as expressions of a shared unconscious. Psychology gave myth a new meaning: stories about the hidden self.

Interpreting Myth

Structure and context

Myth as a system of signs, and as a product of its time

In the 20th century, structuralists looked for the hidden logic that organizes myths, while other scholars insisted on reading each myth in its historical and social setting, including how it treats women.

  • Structuralism: myths as systems of opposites
  • Paris School: myth within Greek society and ritual
  • Contextual and feminist readings
Structuralism
Claude Lévi-Strauss’s method of reading myths as patterns of opposites (nature/culture, raw/cooked) that a culture tries to resolve.
Semiotics: signifier and signified
The study of signs: the signifier is the word or image, the signified the idea it stands for.
Paris School of myth criticism
Jean-Pierre Vernant, Marcel Detienne, and Pierre Vidal-Naquet, who read Greek myth through Greek society, politics, and sacrifice.
Programs of action
The idea that myths model how people should act in their society.
Feminist criticism
Reading myths for how they define and control women, and recovering women’s perspectives.

Theme

Why myths last

Myths survive because every age can find itself in them.

No single theory explains all myths. A story like Oedipus can be ritual, history, psychology, and politics at once, and artists from Athenian vase painters to Moreau have each found something new in it. That openness is why these ancient stories keep returning in art, literature, and film.

  • No single key to all myths
  • Each age reads itself into them
  • Endless reinterpretation in art
