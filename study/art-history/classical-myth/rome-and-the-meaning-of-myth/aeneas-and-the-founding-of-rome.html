Rome and the Meaning of Myth

Aeneas and the Founding of Rome

Virgil’s epic and Rome’s own legends

The Romans adopted the Greek gods but told their own foundation stories, and those stories were about duty, family, and the state. Virgil’s Aeneid, written for the emperor Augustus, makes the Trojan Aeneas the ancestor of Rome and of Augustus’s own family. Roman historians such as Livy then told of Romulus and Remus, the Sabine women, and the heroes of the early Republic.

“Arms, and the man I sing, who, forc’d by fate, And haughty Juno’s unrelenting hate, Expell’d and exil’d, left the Trojan shore.”

Virgil, Aeneid 1.1–3, trans. John Dryden (1697)

Families offered food, wine, and incense here daily. Romans renamed Greek gods for their own: Asclepius became Aesculapius, Persephone Proserpina, Odysseus Ulysses, and Hades Dis or Pluto. This marble temple is one of the oldest surviving in Rome. Aeneas, Anchises, and AscaniusGian Lorenzo Bernini (with Pietro Bernini) · 1618–19Aeneas, son of Venus and the Trojan Anchises, escapes burning Troy carrying his old father, who holds the household gods, and leading his small son Ascanius, also called Iulus. Aeneas Telling Dido of the Misfortunes of TroyPierre-Narcisse Guérin · 1815Aeneas wandered for years: to Thrace, where a bleeding bush warned him away; past the Harpies; to Epirus, where the seer Helenus guided him; and to Sicily, where he saw the blinded Polyphemus. Storms drove him to Carthage, newly founded by Queen Dido. As he told her the story of Troy, she fell in love with him, encouraged by Venus and Cupid, shown here as Ascanius on her lap. Guercino shows her collapsed on the pyre as her sister and attendants rush to her, while Aeneas’s ships sail away in the distance.

Theme

Duty before desire

Aeneas’s greatness lies in giving up what he wants for what he must do.

Unlike Achilles or Odysseus, Aeneas does not fight for personal glory or for his own home. He carries his father and his gods, abandons Dido, and fights a war in Italy because fate and the future Rome require it. Virgil makes that pietas the Roman virtue, and Augustus presented himself as Aeneas’s heir. The poem also mourns the people, like Dido, crushed along the way.

  • Pietas: duty to gods, family, country
  • Personal desire sacrificed to destiny
  • Augustus as Aeneas’s heir

Ara Pacis Augustae: the sacrifice relief13–9 BCEIn Italy Aeneas, guided by the Cumaean Sibyl, visited the underworld, passing the Mourning Fields of tragic lovers and reaching Elysium, where Anchises showed him the future heroes of Rome. Landing in Latium, he found the sign of a white sow, allied with King Evander, and won Lavinia, daughter of King Latinus, by defeating the Rutulian prince Turnus. This relief shows a veiled man sacrificing a sow, traditionally read as Aeneas, though some scholars argue it is Rome’s second king, Numa. Augustus of Prima PortaEarly 1st century CE, after a bronze of ca. 20 BCEAugustus stands like a Greek statue, addressing his troops, his breastplate showing the return of Roman standards from Parthia. At his feet a small Cupid rides a dolphin, a reminder that the Julian family descended from Venus through Aeneas. The statue puts Virgil’s myth into stone: the emperor as the destined heir of Troy. Capitoline WolfDate debated (traditionally 5th century BCE; twins added ca. 1471–1509)In Livy’s history, King Numitor of Alba Longa was overthrown by his brother Amulius, who forced Numitor’s daughter Rhea Silvia to become a Vestal Virgin. Mars fathered twins with her anyway, and Amulius had them thrown into the Tiber. A she-wolf nursed them until the shepherd Faustulus and his wife Larentia raised them; lupa, “wolf,” was also slang for a prostitute, which may explain the story. The wolf’s date is now disputed, with some tests suggesting it was cast in the Middle Ages. Romulus and RemusPeter Paul Rubens · ca. 1615–16Rubens paints the she-wolf licking one twin while the river god Tiber reclines nearby and the shepherd Faustulus arrives. Grown up, the twins restored their grandfather and founded a new city, but in a quarrel Romulus killed Remus and named Rome for himself. After his death he was said to have been taken up to heaven and worshipped as the god Quirinus. The Abduction of the Sabine WomenNicolas Poussin · ca. 1633–34Rome had too few women, so Romulus invited the neighboring Sabines to a festival and, at his signal, the Romans seized their daughters. Later the Sabine king Tatius attacked, helped by the traitor Tarpeia, who opened the citadel and was crushed under the Sabines’ shields. The Sabine women, led by Romulus’s wife Hersilia, ran between the armies and made peace, and the two peoples merged. Poussin shows Romulus giving the signal from the steps at left. The Oath of the HoratiiJacques-Louis David · 1784–85In the war between Rome and Alba Longa, the two cities let three brothers from each side, the Roman Horatii and the Alban Curiatii, fight for them. David shows the Horatii swearing on swords held by their father, while the women, one engaged to a Curiatius, collapse in grief. Only one Horatius survived; when his sister mourned her dead fiancé, he killed her too. LucretiaRembrandt · 1664Sextus Tarquinius, son of Rome’s last king, raped the virtuous Lucretia, wife of Tarquinius Collatinus. She told her husband and father what had happened and stabbed herself. Lucius Junius Brutus swore on her blood to drive out the kings, and in 509 BCE the Romans founded the Republic. Rembrandt shows her in the moment before the blow, dagger raised, face full of sorrow.

Theme

Myth as Roman history

Rome’s legends taught citizens what Rome expected of them.

Roman stories are set in Rome itself and told as history. They praise self-sacrifice for the state, punish betrayal, and often turn on the bodies of women, the Sabines, Tarpeia, Lucretia, whose suffering changes the course of politics. Later revolutions, from France to America, borrowed these legends as models of civic virtue.

  • Told as history, set in Rome
  • Duty and sacrifice for the state
  • Women’s fates drive political change
