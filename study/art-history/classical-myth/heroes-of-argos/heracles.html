Heroes of Argos

Heracles

The greatest hero and his twelve labors

Heracles, “the glory of Hera,” was the strongest of the Greek heroes and the only one worshipped as a god across the Greek world. Son of Zeus and the mortal Alcmena, he was persecuted by Hera from birth, driven mad, and forced to perform twelve impossible labors. His life mixes superhuman deeds with violence, appetite, and suffering, and it ends with his rise to Olympus.

  • Son of Zeus and Alcmena; enemy of Hera
  • Twelve labors for King Eurystheus
  • Mortal hero who becomes a god
Alcmena and Amphitryon
Heracles’ mortal mother and her husband; Amphitryon fathered his twin, Iphicles.
Eurystheus
King of Tiryns and Mycenae, Heracles’ cousin, who set the twelve labors.
Labors, deeds, side-deeds
The labors (athloi) were the twelve tasks set by Eurystheus; deeds (praxeis) were other adventures; side-deeds (parerga) happened along the way.

Farnese HerculesAfter Lysippos · Roman copy, early 3rd century CE, of a Greek bronze of the 4th century BCEThe hero leans exhausted on his club, draped with the skin of the Nemean lion. Behind his back he holds the golden apples of the Hesperides, so this is the end of his labors, not the beginning. Hera sent snakes to kill him in his cradle, but the infant strangled them; later she drove him mad, and he killed his first wife, Megara, and their children. To be cleansed of this blood guilt he served Eurystheus. The Choice of HerculesAnnibale Carracci · 1596In a story told by the Greek writer Prodicus, the young Heracles meets two women at a crossroads: Pleasure offers an easy life, while Virtue points to a steep, rocky path that leads to glory. Heracles chooses Virtue. Carracci set the scene above the desk of a young cardinal, as a reminder to choose duty over pleasure. Hercules and the HydraAntonio del Pollaiuolo · ca. 1475The first labors cleared the land of monsters: the Nemean lion, whose skin Heracles wore, and the nine-headed Hydra of Lerna, which grew two heads for each one cut off until his nephew Iolaus burned the stumps. Then came the Golden Hind of Artemis, the Erymanthian Boar, the filthy stables of King Augeas, and the man-eating Stymphalian Birds. Pollaiuolo, an expert in anatomy, gives the hero taut, straining muscles against a wide Tuscan landscape. Krater with Heracles wrestling AntaeusEuphronios · Athenian, ca. 515–510 BCEOn his way to the apples of the Hesperides, Heracles fought Antaeus, a giant son of Earth who grew stronger every time he touched the ground. Heracles lifted him into the air and crushed him. Euphronios contrasts the neat, controlled hero with the wild-haired giant whose eyes roll in pain, a picture of civilization defeating savagery. Metope with Heracles, Atlas, and Athenaca. 470–456 BCEThe later labors took Heracles to the edges of the world: the Cretan Bull, the man-eating Mares of Diomedes, the belt of the Amazon queen Hippolyta, the cattle of the monster Geryon, and the golden apples of the Hesperides. For the apples, Heracles held up the sky while Atlas fetched them. Here Atlas returns with the apples while Athena quietly helps Heracles bear the weight. Amphora with Heracles and CerberusAndokides Painter · Athenian, ca. 520–510 BCEThe last and hardest labor was to bring Cerberus, the three-headed guard dog of the underworld, up to the living. Heracles crouches and reaches out to the dog almost gently, holding a chain, while Athena stands behind him. When Heracles brought Cerberus to Eurystheus, the frightened king hid in a storage jar. Going down to Hades and returning was the hero’s ultimate victory over death.

Theme

Labors that make the world safe

Heracles clears the world of monsters so civilization can grow.

Most of the labors pit the hero against wild beasts and savage beings at the edges of the known world. Each victory makes the land usable for farming, travel, and cities. Greeks across the Mediterranean claimed Heracles had visited them, and the Temple of Zeus at Olympia put all twelve labors on display as models of effort rewarded.

  • Monsters and savages defeated
  • Hero as founder and civilizer
  • Displayed at Olympia as a model

Deianira Abducted by the Centaur NessusGuido Reni · 1620–21Heracles’ second wife, Deianira, was carried off by the centaur Nessus, who offered to ferry her across a river; Heracles shot him with an arrow dipped in the Hydra’s poison. The dying centaur told Deianira to keep his blood as a love charm. Years later, jealous of a captive princess, she sent Heracles a robe soaked in it, and the poison burned his flesh. In agony he built his own funeral pyre on Mount Oeta. The Apotheosis of HerculesFrançois Lemoyne · 1733–36As Heracles’ body burned on the pyre, his mortal part was consumed and his divine part rose to Olympus. There he was reconciled with Hera and married her daughter Hebe, goddess of youth. Lemoyne fills the ceiling with more than a hundred figures welcoming the hero into heaven. Rulers loved the theme because it promised that great labors earn immortality.

Theme

The flawed hero

Heracles’ strength is matched by his excesses and his suffering.

Heracles kills his own family in madness, eats and drinks enormously, rages against gods and men, and dies through a wife’s mistaken jealousy. Greeks laughed at him in comedies and honored him in temples. Later Christians and moralists read his life as a struggle of virtue against appetite that ended in heaven, which is why rulers and churches alike used his image.

  • Madness, appetite, violence
  • Comic glutton and revered god
  • Read later as virtue rewarded
