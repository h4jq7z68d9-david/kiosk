Heroes of Argos

Perseus and the Heroes of Argos

The pattern of the hero, Medusa, and Andromeda

Greek heroes are mortal sons of gods whose lives follow a familiar pattern: a strange birth, a dangerous quest, help from the gods, monsters defeated, and a reward at the end. Perseus, the great hero of Argos, fits the pattern almost perfectly. His family tree also links many myths of the Argive plain, from Io and the Danaïds to his great-grandson Heracles.

  • Heroes follow a shared pattern of folktale motifs
  • Perseus: hero of Argos and ancestor of Heracles
  • Argive line: Io → Danaïds → Danaë → Perseus
Folktale motifs in heroic myth
Divine parent, miraculous birth, great strength, a loyal companion, impossible tasks set by an enemy, a broken taboo, a dangerous woman resisted, a quest (often to the underworld), help from gods, return and atonement, a great reward, and a hero’s funeral or deification.
Inachus and Melia
The river god of Argos and an ash-tree nymph, parents of Io in some versions.
Epaphus
Son of Zeus and Io, born in Egypt; ancestor of Danaus, Acrisius, and Perseus.
Acrisius
King of Argos who locked his daughter Danaë away because an oracle said her son would kill him.

Theme

The pattern of the hero

Heroic myths are built from the same parts, rearranged.

Perseus has a god for a father and a miraculous birth, is sent on an impossible quest by a hostile king, receives magical gifts from the gods, kills a monster, rescues a princess, and returns to punish his enemy. Heracles, Theseus, and Jason follow versions of the same plan. Recognizing the pattern helps explain why these stories feel familiar and why they were easy to retell.

  • Divine birth, quest, monster, reward
  • Help from gods and magical objects
  • Same plan behind Heracles, Theseus, Jason

Perseus and the GraiaeEdward Burne-Jones · ca. 1875–78Danaë and the infant Perseus were set adrift in a chest and washed ashore on the island of Seriphos, where the fisherman Dictys took them in. King Polydectes, wanting Danaë, sent Perseus to fetch the head of the Gorgon Medusa. To find her, Perseus seized the single eye and tooth shared by the Graiae, the Gorgons’ gray-haired sisters, until they gave him directions. Burne-Jones shows him snatching the eye as the three blind sisters grope for it. Metopes from Temple C at SelinusGreek (Sicily), ca. 540–530 BCEThe center metope shows Perseus cutting off Medusa’s head while Athena stands by and Medusa clutches the winged horse Pegasus. The Gorgons were three sisters: Stheno (“strength”), Euryale (“far-leaper”), and the mortal Medusa (“ruler”). Perseus used the gifts of the gods, winged sandals, a cap of invisibility, a curved sword, and a bag, and looked only at her reflection to avoid being turned to stone. From her severed neck sprang Pegasus and Chrysaor, her sons by Poseidon. MedusaCaravaggio · ca. 1597–98Even after death, Medusa’s head kept its power, and Perseus gave it to Athena, who set it on her aegis. Images of the Gorgon’s face were placed on shields, armor, and temples to frighten away enemies and evil. Caravaggio paints the head at the instant of beheading, still screaming, on a real convex shield, so the viewer, like Perseus, sees her only as a reflection.

Theme

The face that protects

The monster’s deadly face becomes a weapon against danger.

Medusa’s gaze kills, but once she is defeated her face turns into protection. Greeks painted Gorgon faces on shields and roof tiles, and Athena wears one on her breastplate. Later artists from Cellini to Caravaggio used the severed head as a symbol of power over enemies, and modern writers have read her as an image of fear of female power.

  • Deadly gaze turned into protection
  • On shields, armor, temples, and Athena’s aegis
  • A symbol of power over enemies
Apotropaic
Meant to turn away evil or bad luck, like a Gorgon face on a shield.

Perseus with the Head of MedusaBenvenuto Cellini · 1545–54Perseus stands on Medusa’s body holding up her head, blood pouring from her neck in curling bronze streams. Cellini, a goldsmith, cast the statue in one daring pour that he described in his autobiography. Set near Donatello’s Judith and Michelangelo’s David, both symbols of the Florentine republic, the Perseus told citizens that Medici rule had beheaded its enemies. Perseus and AndromedaTitian · 1554–56On his way home, Perseus found the Ethiopian princess Andromeda chained to a rock by the sea. Her mother, Queen Cassiopeia, had boasted that she was more beautiful than the Nereids, the sea nymphs, and Poseidon sent a sea monster to punish the kingdom; an oracle said only Andromeda’s sacrifice would end it. Titian shows Perseus diving down from the sky while the pale, chained princess twists away from the monster below. Perseus Freeing AndromedaPeter Paul Rubens · ca. 1620–22Rubens skips the fight and shows the aftermath: Perseus, crowned by Victory, gently frees Andromeda while cupids lead Pegasus and the dead monster lies below. Perseus later used Medusa’s head to turn Polydectes and his court to stone, returned the gifts of the gods, and, as foretold, accidentally killed his grandfather Acrisius with a discus. He then ruled Tiryns, and his granddaughter Alcmena’s son was Heracles.

Theme

The girl’s tragedy

Many myths follow a young woman through loss, suffering, and rescue.

Io, Danaë, and Andromeda all follow a pattern scholars call the girl’s tragedy: a princess is separated from her home, loved or threatened by a god or monster, punished or exposed, and finally rescued, often bearing a hero son. The story mirrors a Greek girl’s life, leaving her father’s house for marriage. In art it gave painters a reason to show female suffering and rescue.

  • Leaving home, threat, punishment, rescue
  • Mirrors the move from girl to bride
  • Io, Danaë, Andromeda
