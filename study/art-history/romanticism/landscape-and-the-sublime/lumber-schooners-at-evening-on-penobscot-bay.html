Landscape and the Sublime

Fitz Henry Lane

Lumber Schooners at Evening on Penobscot Bay

1863

Oil on canvas, 62.6 × 96.8 cm. National Gallery of Art, Washington

A schooner stacked with lumber rests on a calm bay in Maine as the evening sky turns pink. The tiny crew and a far-off ship leave most of the canvas to sky and water.

  • First exhibited in 1963–64 in Maine and Its Artists, shown at Colby College, the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, and the Whitney.
  • Look at the four small sailors on deck working the sails.
  • Lane used thin, see-through glazes instead of thick paint, so his brushwork is hard to see.
  • The National Gallery sets this stillness against the more explosive style of Cole and Church. It links Lane's quiet, self-effacing method to Emerson's idea that artists should let nature speak through them.
  • Given to the National Gallery in 1980 by Mr. and Mrs. Francis W. Hatch, Sr.
Luminism
A later name for American landscapes of about 1850–1875 marked by still water, soft light and hidden brushwork.
