Myths of Death

The underworld and its visitors

For most Greeks, the dead became weak, shadowy images of themselves in the gloomy realm of Hades, whatever kind of life they had lived. Only a few great sinners were singled out for punishment and a few favored heroes for paradise. Over the centuries, mystery cults, philosophers, and Roman poets turned this shadowland into a place of judgment and reward, a picture that passed into Christian ideas of hell and heaven.

“The gates of hell are open night and day; Smooth the descent, and easy is the way”

Virgil, Aeneid 6, trans. John Dryden (1697)
  • Hades: shadowy afterlife for most
  • Punishment for a few, paradise for fewer
  • Later writers add judgment and reward
Soul (psyche, anima)
The breath or life-spirit that leaves the body at death; psyche in Greek, anima in Latin.
Hades
“The unseen one,” the god and his realm; the Romans called the god Pluto, “the enricher.”
Orcus
A Roman name for the underworld and its god, “the place that confines.”
Eidolon
The image or shade of a dead person, a thin copy of the living.
Anthesteria
Athenian spring festival of new wine when the spirits of the dead were believed to roam, sometimes compared to Halloween.
