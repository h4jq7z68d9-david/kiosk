After Alkamenes

Miniature herm of Hermes

Roman, 1st–2nd century CE, after a Greek original of ca. 430 BCE

Marble, 4.6 cm (1¾ in.) high · Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York

Made for: A tiny Roman copy of a herm, the square pillar with a god’s head and male genitals that Greeks set up at doorways, crossroads, and boundaries to protect them.

Hermes, son of Zeus and the nymph Maia, protects travelers, merchants, and thieves, and guides souls of the dead to the underworld. Greeks set up herms, pillars topped with his bearded head, outside houses and at crossroads to ward off harm. This old-fashioned, bearded style copies a famous herm by the sculptor Alkamenes that stood at the entrance to the Athenian Acropolis.

  • Herm = boundary pillar with Hermes’s head
  • Protects doors, roads, and crossings
  • Guide of souls (psychopompos)
Herm
A square stone pillar topped with a head of Hermes, set at boundaries and doorways.
Apotropaion
An object meant to turn away evil, such as a herm or a Gorgon’s face.
Psychopompos
“Guide of souls,” Hermes’s role leading the dead to Hades.
Caduceus
Hermes’s herald’s staff entwined with two snakes; one story links it to snakes the seer Tiresias struck apart.
Autolycus
Son of Hermes, master thief, and grandfather of Odysseus.
