Hydria with Zeus fighting Typhon

Chalcidian, ca. 540–530 BCE

Black-figure terracotta · Staatliche Antikensammlungen, Munich

Made for: A hydria, a three-handled jar used to carry water; this one, made by Greek potters in southern Italy, was found in an Etruscan grave.

Angry at the defeat of her Titan children, Gaea mated with Tartarus and gave birth to Typhon, a winged giant with a hundred snake heads. In the Typhonomachy Zeus fought him with thunderbolts and finally buried him under Mount Etna in Sicily, whose eruptions were said to be the monster’s fire. Here Zeus strides forward with a thunderbolt at the winged, snake-legged Typhon.

Typhonomachy
Zeus’s battle with the monster Typhon, the second fight for control of the world.
Typhon (Typhoeus)
A monstrous, winged, snake-legged son of Gaea and Tartarus, defeated by Zeus.

“And in the bitterness of his anger Zeus cast him into wide Tartarus.”

Hesiod, Theogony 868, trans. Evelyn-White
