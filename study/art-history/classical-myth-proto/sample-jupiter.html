Jupiter of Smyrna

2nd century CE

Marble, 270 cm (about 8 ft 10 in) high · Louvre, Paris (Ma 13)

This over-life-size marble figure was carved in the Roman province of Asia in the 2nd century CE, possibly after a Greek original about three centuries older. The French scholar Antoine Galland recorded it at Smyrna (modern İzmir) in March 1680, when it belonged to a Dutch merchant, and it was bought for Louis XIV that year. The sculptor cut the hair and beard with a drill, leaving deep channels that hold shadow and make the curls read from a distance.

Much of what we see is 17th-century work. The royal sculptor Pierre Granier rebuilt the damaged statue in 1686, adding the shoulder and raised right arm with its thunderbolt, the nose, the feet and the base. It stood on the Allée Royale in the gardens of Versailles from 1687 and moved to the Louvre after the Revolution. Because the arm and thunderbolt are modern, the identification is not certain; the Louvre also records Asclepius, the bearded god of healing, as a possibility.

The subject

Zeus, called Jupiter by the Romans, is king of the gods and god of the sky and thunder. He took power by overthrowing his father Cronus, and as guardian of oaths and hospitality he stands for order and justice, though his many love affairs produced most of the demigod heroes. Sculptors showed him as a mature, bearded man, bare-chested, with scepter and thunderbolt.

  • Look for the deep drill work in the beard and hair, typical of 2nd-century Roman carving.
  • The arm, thunderbolt and feet are Granier’s 1686 restoration, not ancient.
  • The name “Jupiter” rests partly on the restored thunderbolt.
